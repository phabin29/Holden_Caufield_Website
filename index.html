PROLOGUE

A Boy, A City,
and a Great Deal of Rain

In December of 1949, somewhere in a private sanitarium in California, a seventeen-year-old named Holden Caulfield begins to tell his story. He has just been expelled from his fourth school. He has just lost three days wandering New York City, terrified of going home. He has just, in his own words, come close to cracking up entirely. What follows is one of the loneliest, funniest, most unbearably tender voices in American letters — the voice of a boy who loved the world so much, and so badly, that he could not figure out how to live inside it. These ten photographs are not illustrations. They are an attempt to stand where Holden stood and see what he saw.

— New York, after dark —

N° 01 / 10

Setting · Alienation

New York City,
Going Nowhere

Manhattan is not a backdrop in this novel — it is Holden Caulfield's accomplice and his prison. After three days of wandering, he has spoken to cab drivers, hat-check girls, nuns, and prostitutes, and still nobody knows his name. The city's electric brightness only deepens his invisibility. A million lit windows, and not a single one waiting for him.

"It was lousy in the park. It was so cold you could see your breath coming out of your mouth." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 20

— The lagoon, Central Park South —

N° 02 / 10

Curiosity · Impermanence

The Ducks &
Where They Go

Twice Holden asks a cab driver where the Central Park ducks go when the lagoon freezes over. The cabbies think he's joking, or crazy. He is neither. The question is the whole novel in miniature: where does anything tender go when the world freezes around it? It is a child's question — which is why it matters so much that he, nearly an adult, is still asking it.

"You know those ducks in that lagoon right near Central Park South? Do you happen to know where they go, the ducks, when it gets all frozen over?" — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 9

— A phony kind of evening —

N° 03 / 10

Alienation · The Phonies

Wet Asphalt,
The Phony World

"Phony" appears thirty-five times in the novel. The word is the tip of a much larger grief: a revulsion at the performances the adult world demands. Every polished surface, to Holden, hides a con. A rain-slick street at night is this book's natural habitat — beautiful, gleaming, and slippery, a place where the real and the counterfeit reflect each other back perfectly.

"One of the biggest reasons I left Elkton Hills was because I was surrounded by phonies. They were coming in the goddam window." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 2

— Phoebe, on the carousel, in the rain —

N° 04 / 10

Innocence · Redemption

The Carousel,
Grabbing for the Ring

The climax of the novel arrives not with a gunshot or a revelation but with a child riding a painted horse in the rain. Phoebe leans out, reaching for the brass ring, and Holden — for the first time in three hundred pages — lets go of his fear. She might fall. That is the point. He finally understands that you cannot catch everyone, and that trying to is its own kind of falling.

"The thing with kids is, if they want to grab for the gold ring, you have to let them do it, and not say anything. If they fall off, they fall off." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 25

"I felt so lonesome, all of a sudden.
I almost wished I was dead."

INTERMISSION

"People never notice anything."
— Holden Caulfield

— Pencey Prep, the morning after —

N° 05 / 10

Failure · Departure

The Empty Hall,
Expelled Again

Pencey is the fourth school to send Holden packing. He is not stupid; he has simply refused to rehearse a life that feels like an elaborate lie. Institutional corridors — with their waxed floors and stamped permission slips — are where the adult world politely tells him he does not belong. It is a rejection, but it is also, in its way, an accurate verdict on both parties.

"You never saw so many phonies in all your life, everybody smoking their ears off and talking about the play so that everybody could hear and know how sharp they were." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 17

— A sandwich, a coffee, nobody home —

N° 06 / 10

Loneliness · Longing

A Table for One,
Dialing Jane

No one in American literature is worse at being alone, or better at choosing it. Holden walks into phone booths intending to call Jane Gallagher a dozen times and walks out having called no one. He sits at counters and in hotel bars hoping to be recognized, then sabotages every exchange. Loneliness, for him, is both a terror and a kind of fidelity — the only thing he cannot betray.

"I felt like giving somebody a buzz. I nearly called Jane Gallagher. I even started dialing. Then I hung up." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 9

— Museum of Natural History, unchanged —

N° 07 / 10

Memory · Permanence

Behind the Glass,
A World That Waits

Holden's favorite place is the Museum of Natural History, because nothing inside it ever changes. The Eskimo forever fishes. The deer forever drinks. Only Holden is different each time he walks in. His yearning for unchanging things is the exact mirror of his dread of growing up. The dioramas are a fantasy of a world where innocence is preserved behind glass — which is to say, a world without him.

"The best thing in that museum was that everything always stayed right where it was. Nobody'd move." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 16

— The red hunting hat —

N° 08 / 10

Identity · Armor

The Red Hunting Hat,
A Boy's Flag

He bought it in New York the morning he lost the fencing team's equipment on the subway. He wears it backwards, peak turned around, when he is alone or afraid or writing something. It is his shield against the phonies, his private emblem, the one article of clothing in which he is unmistakably himself. At the end of the novel, he gives it to Phoebe — the most tender gesture he knows how to make.

"I put my red hunting hat on, and turned the peak around to the back, the way I liked it, and then I yelled, 'Sleep tight, ya morons!'" — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 7

"Certain things they should stay the way they are.
You ought to be able to stick them in one of those big glass cases
and just leave them alone."

— A wall at Phoebe's school —

N° 09 / 10

Protection · Futility

The Scratched Wall,
Rubbing it Out

In Phoebe's school stairwell, Holden finds an obscenity scrawled on the wall. He scrubs it off. Then finds another. Then another, etched so deep he cannot erase it. His fury is not moral but protective — he cannot bear the thought of a child reading it. This is who Holden really is: not a rebel, not a cynic, but an exhausted custodian of other people's innocence.

"If you had a million years to do it in, you couldn't rub out even half the [signs] in the world. It's impossible." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 25

— The field of rye, a dream —

N° 10 / 10

Dream · Salvation

The Field of Rye,
The Catcher's Dream

Born from a lyric he misheard as a boy, Holden's great vision of his own future is this: thousands of children playing in a vast field of rye beside a cliff, and him — only him — standing at the edge to catch the ones who run too close. It is not a career. It is a prayer. It is the only life he can imagine that would be worth having. And it is, of course, impossible. That is why he needs it.

"I keep picturing all these little kids playing some game in this big field of rye… I'd just be the catcher in the rye and all." — Catcher in the Rye, Ch. 22
❖ ❖ ❖

A Boy at the Edge
of the Cliff

Holden Caulfield is not a rebel. He is a mourner. He mourns his brother Allie, and a sister who is still young enough to ride carousels, and the strange country of childhood he can feel receding even as he describes it to us. These photographs do not tell his story. They stand where he stood — in the cold, in the rain, at the edge of the rye — and look the way he looked. Which is to say: with a kind of ferocious love for a world that refused to stay still.
